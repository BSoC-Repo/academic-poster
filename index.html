Section 1

What is an academic poster?

An academic poster is a single large sheet, usually A0 or A1, that summarises a piece of research visually. It is designed to be read by someone standing one to two metres away, in a few minutes, often while you stand beside it and talk them through the work. A good poster is a conversation starter, not a miniature dissertation.

Where posters are used

  • Conferences: poster sessions let many researchers share work at once, and delegates browse and stop at what interests them.
  • Assessment: many final-year projects are assessed partly through a poster and a short oral defence.
  • Showcases: degree shows and employer events, where visitors may not be specialists in your topic.

Who is the audience?

  • Passers-by decide in about five seconds, from the title alone, whether to stop.
  • Interested non-specialists scan headings and figures for one or two minutes.
  • Specialists and markers read closely and ask about method and limitations.

Your design has to work for all three, in that order.

Section 2

Poster anatomy

Most research posters use the same ten parts. Select each part of the model poster to see its purpose, a recommended word count and the mistakes markers see most often. The numbers show the usual reading order in a three-column landscape layout.

Tip: use Tab to move between sections and Enter or Space to select one.

1. Title

Purpose
Tells a passer-by what the project is about and why they should stop. It is the only part most people will read.
Recommended length
10 to 15 words at most, readable from about five metres.
Common mistakes
  • A long, jargon-heavy title copied from the report
  • ALL CAPITALS, which are slower to read
  • Text too small or squeezed between logos

2. Authors and affiliation

Purpose
Shows who did the work, where, and how to get in touch. Include your name, programme, institution, supervisor and a contact email or QR code.
Recommended length
Two or three short lines. Not counted in the word budget.
Common mistakes
  • Leaving out the supervisor or the institution
  • Stretched, pixelated or oversized logos
  • No way for a visitor to follow up

3. Abstract or summary

Purpose
Gives the whole story in a few sentences: problem, approach, key finding, implication. Some briefs and conferences ask you to leave it out because the poster is itself a summary, so check.
Recommended length
50 to 100 words.
Common mistakes
  • Pasting the 250-word abstract from the report
  • Repeating the introduction word for word
  • No finding stated

4. Introduction

Purpose
Sets out the context, the problem or gap, and why it matters, with two or three key citations.
Recommended length
80 to 120 words, ideally as three or four bullet points.
Common mistakes
  • A condensed literature review
  • No clear statement of the gap
  • A string of citations with no argument

5. Aims or research questions

Purpose
States exactly what the project set out to find or build. Everything in the findings should answer these.
Recommended length
30 to 50 words: one aim, or one to three numbered research questions.
Common mistakes
  • Vague aims such as "to explore" with no object
  • Too many questions
  • Aims that the findings never return to

6. Methodology

Purpose
Explains what you did so the reader can judge how far to trust the findings: design, participants or data, tools, analysis and ethics. A flow diagram often does this better than text.
Recommended length
80 to 120 words, plus a diagram where it helps.
Common mistakes
  • A step-by-step diary of the project
  • No sample size or data source
  • No mention of ethical approval where people were involved

7. Findings

Purpose
Presents the evidence that answers your research questions. This is the heart of the poster and should take the most space, usually the centre.
Recommended length
100 to 150 words, plus two or three well-labelled figures.
Common mistakes
  • Tables of raw data
  • Charts with no axis labels, units or caption
  • Reporting everything instead of selecting what answers the questions

8. Discussion or conclusion

Purpose
Says what the findings mean, how they relate to the literature, their limitations and what should happen next. End on a clear take-home message.
Recommended length
80 to 120 words.
Common mistakes
  • Restating the findings without interpreting them
  • Claiming more than the evidence supports
  • No limitations

9. References

Purpose
Gives full details of every source cited on the poster, in BFC Harvard (Cite Them Right) style, so readers can find them.
Recommended length
Five to eight key sources. Not counted in the word budget. Text can be smaller than the body but should still be readable up close.
Common mistakes
  • Inconsistent formatting
  • Sources cited in the text but missing from the list
  • Bare URLs instead of full references

10. Acknowledgements

Purpose
Thanks the people and organisations who made the work possible: supervisor, participants, industry partners, funders.
Recommended length
20 to 40 words.
Common mistakes
  • Leaving it out altogether
  • Long personal thanks
  • Naming participants who were promised anonymity

Section 3

Design principles

Design is not decoration. Each choice below either helps a viewer find and understand your key message or gets in the way. Open each heading in turn.

3.1 Visual hierarchy and reading flow

Visual hierarchy is the order in which the eye notices things. Size, weight, colour and position signal importance, so the title should be the largest element, followed by section headings, key figures and then body text. Aim for three or four clear levels and use them consistently.

Reading flow is the path a viewer follows. Make it obvious, and number your sections if there is any doubt.

Squint test. Step back from the screen and squint. You should still be able to see the title, where each section starts and which figure matters most.

3.2 Typography
  • Use a clean sans-serif family for most text, for example Arial, Calibri, Helvetica or Open Sans. They stay legible at a distance.
  • Use no more than two font families: one for headings and one for body, or a single family in different weights.
  • Left-align body text with a ragged right edge. Avoid full justification and centred paragraphs.
  • Use bold for emphasis. Avoid underlining, italics for long passages and blocks of capitals.
  • Keep lines to roughly 40 to 65 characters and line spacing at 1.2 to 1.5.

Check your page size first. Set the slide or artboard to the real print size before you start. A1 is about 71% of A0 in each direction, so a poster designed at A1 and printed at A0 has every font size enlarged by about 141%. If your module provides a template, use its sizes.

3.3 Colour
  • Limit the palette to two or three colours plus neutrals: one dominant colour, one accent for emphasis, and dark text on a light background.
  • Contrast. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines ask for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text and for meaningful parts of charts (World Wide Web Consortium, 2023). Aim for 7:1 for body text where you can.
  • Colour-blind safe choices. About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a colour vision deficiency. Avoid relying on red against green, and never use colour as the only signal: add labels, patterns or different marker shapes.
  • Avoid photographs or gradients behind text.

3.4 White space, alignment and grids
  • White space (empty space of any colour) separates sections and gives the eye somewhere to rest. As a rule of thumb, leave around 30 to 40% of the poster empty.
  • Build on a grid: equal column widths, equal gutters (about 20 to 30 mm at A0) and an outer margin of at least 25 mm so nothing is trimmed in printing.
  • Align every text box and figure to the column edges. Switch on guides and snapping in PowerPoint, Canva, Figma or Illustrator.
  • Keep spacing consistent: the same gap above every heading and the same padding inside every box.
  • Prefer space to boxes and borders. Heavy outlines around every section add clutter.
3.5 Figures, charts, tables and images
  • Resolution. Images need about 300 pixels per inch at printed size, and never less than 150. An image printed 20 cm wide needs roughly 2,400 pixels across. Screenshots and images copied from the web usually print blurred.
  • Use vector formats (SVG, PDF, EMF) for charts, diagrams and logos so they stay sharp at any size.
  • Choose the simplest chart that shows the point. Remove gridlines, 3D effects and legends where direct labels will do.
  • Label everything: axis titles with units, readable tick labels (at least caption size) and data labels for key values.
  • Number and caption every figure and table (Figure 1, Table 1) and refer to each one in the text. On a poster the caption should state the message, for example "Figure 2. Defect density fell by 31% after TDD was adopted".
  • Tables: no more than about four columns and six rows. If it is bigger, turn it into a chart.
  • Credit and permission. Cite the source of any image you did not create, and use only images you have the right to use.
3.6 Referencing in BFC Harvard (Cite Them Right) on a poster

A poster is academic work, so the same rules of integrity apply as in a report. What changes is the quantity.

  • Use author-date citations in the text, following Cite Them Right (Pears and Shields, 2022): (Okabe and Ito, 2008) or Okabe and Ito (2008). Write "and" in full, not "&". Name up to three authors; for four or more use the first author followed by et al. Add a page number for a direct quotation: (Pears and Shields, 2022, p. 12).
  • Cite selectively: the five to eight sources that matter most. Every citation on the poster must appear in the reference list, and the list should contain nothing that is not cited.
  • Put the reference list in the bottom right, in alphabetical order by first author, with a hanging indent. It may be in smaller text than the body.
  • Cite sources for figures and data in the caption, for example "Adapted from Smith (2023)".
  • If you run out of space, ask your tutor whether a QR code linking to a fuller reference list is acceptable. Do not assume it is.

Patterns

Journal article: Surname, Initial. and Surname, Initial. (Year) 'Title of article', Title of Journal, volume(issue), pp. page–page. Available at: https://doi.org/xxxx

Book: Surname, Initial. (Year) Title of book. Edition (if not the first). Place of publication: Publisher.

Web page: Surname, Initial. or Organisation (Year) Title of web page. Available at: https://xxxx (Accessed: Day Month Year).

Section 4

Writing for posters

Condensing months of research into a few hundred words is the hardest part of making a poster. The aim is not to shrink your report but to select from it.

Start from the key message

  1. Write your key message in one sentence of 25 words or fewer.
  2. List the three pieces of evidence a sceptical reader would need to believe it.
  3. Choose one figure for each piece of evidence.
  4. Add only the context and method needed to make sense of those figures.
  5. Cut everything else and keep it for the conversation.

Techniques for cutting words

  • Use bullet points of one or two lines in place of paragraphs.
  • Write headings as findings: "TDD teams shipped fewer defects" says more than "Results".
  • Use the active voice and short sentences of under 20 words.
  • Replace descriptions with diagrams: a flowchart for method, a chart for results.
  • Remove filler ("it is important to note that"), hedging and repeated definitions.
  • Define essential abbreviations once and avoid the rest.

Worked example

This example is fictional and is used only to show the technique.

Before: taken from the report (86 words)

In order to investigate the research question, a quasi-experimental study was undertaken in which a total of twelve second-year student teams were observed over the course of one twelve-week semester. Six of the teams were instructed to make use of test-driven development practices, whereas the remaining six teams continued to utilise their existing test-last approach. Defect data was subsequently collected from the issue trackers of each of the teams and was then normalised by the number of thousand lines of code in order to calculate defect density.

After: on the poster (33 words)

  • Design: quasi-experiment, 12 student teams, 12 weeks
  • Groups: 6 teams used test-driven development (TDD), 6 used test-last
  • Measure: defects per 1,000 lines of code, from issue trackers

The same information, in under half the words, and it can be scanned in five seconds.

Section 5

Evaluate two example posters

Below are two schematic posters presenting the same fictional project. Grey lines stand for text. Rate each poster against the eight criteria, then compare your ratings with the tutor's. In the taught session, do this in pairs and be ready to justify one rating where you disagreed.

Poster A

Description of Poster A. The title runs to three lines of small text, centred and squeezed between three logos. There are four columns of different widths, filled edge to edge with dense paragraphs in a small font and with almost no margins. Section headings are yellow on white and centred. The only figure is a small pie chart in red and green with no labels or caption. All ten standard sections are present, including a full reference list.

Poster B

Description of Poster B. A dark blue band across the top holds a large one-line title in white with the author line beneath. There are three equal columns with generous margins. Dark blue headings sit above short blocks of bullet points. A large bar chart in blue and orange dominates the centre column, with a caption and an orange call-out box stating the key finding. A second, smaller chart in the right-hand column has no caption or axis labels. All ten standard sections are present.
